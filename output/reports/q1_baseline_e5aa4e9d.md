# Imagining the Future: The 1939 New York World's Fair and Its Visions of Urban Life

## Introduction
The 1939 New York World's Fair was a monumental event that sought to project a vision of the future, particularly in urban living and technological advancements. Amidst the backdrop of the Great Depression, the fair aimed to inspire hope and optimism for a better tomorrow. Central to this vision was the Futurama exhibit, designed by Norman Bel Geddes and sponsored by General Motors, which showcased an idealized future of automated highways and sprawling suburbs. This report explores how the fair imagined the future city and everyday life, examining the differing interests of its organizers and corporate sponsors.

## The Vision of Futurama
Futurama was the main attraction at the 1939 New York World's Fair, hailed as the 'number-one hit show' of the event [D19]. It presented a model of America set in the year 1960, featuring a vast landscape with over 500,000 buildings and 50,000 cars, all interconnected by an automated highway system [D19]. The exhibit emphasized key themes such as safety, comfort, speed, and economy, reflecting the aspirations of a nation eager for recovery and progress [D19].

The exhibit offered a simulated aerial journey through this model, allowing spectators to experience the scale and intricacies of urban design in a way that was immersive and engaging [D19]. Advanced technologies were highlighted, including remote-controlled vehicles and flying machines, all contributing to a utopian vision of the future [D19]. This portrayal was particularly appealing to an audience still grappling with the economic hardships of the Great Depression, as it promised a brighter and more prosperous future [D19].

## The Role of Corporations
The sponsorship of Futurama by General Motors Corporation played a crucial role in shaping the exhibit's vision. General Motors, a leading automaker, was keen to promote its vision of a future dominated by automobiles and expansive road networks [D19]. The company's preference for Bel Geddes' design over their original plans stemmed from its broader appeal to the public, aligning with their corporate interests in promoting car culture and infrastructure development [D19].

The influence of corporate interests on the fair's vision is evident in the way Futurama was designed to showcase not just a futuristic city, but also the potential for economic growth through automobile dependency. This vision directly contributed to the Federal-Aid Highway Act, which laid the groundwork for the development of the Interstate Highway System in the United States [D19]. Thus, while the fair aimed to inspire hope and innovation, it also served the interests of corporate sponsors like General Motors, who sought to solidify their market position in an emerging automotive economy.

## The Contrast with Other Exhibits
While Futurama captured the imagination of fairgoers, other exhibits, such as those presented by Ford Motor Company, did not resonate as strongly. Ford's exhibit received a significantly lower interest rating compared to Futurama, indicating that the public was more captivated by the vision of a fully automated future than by traditional automotive displays [D19]. This contrast highlights how the fair's organizers and corporate sponsors had differing visions of the future, with some focusing on innovation and automation while others relied on established narratives of car culture.

## Democracity and the Theme Center
At the heart of the 1939 World's Fair was the Theme Center, which included the Trylon and Perisphere, designed by Wallace Harrison and J. Andre Fouilhoux [D48]. The Perisphere housed a diorama called Democracity, which depicted a utopian city of the future designed by Henry Dreyfuss [D48]. Visitors experienced this vision from a moving sidewalk, enhancing the immersive quality of the exhibit [D48].

Democracity showcased a vision of urban living that aligned with the ideals of the fair, emphasizing community and progress. However, it also reflected the interests of its corporate sponsors, as the design incorporated elements that promoted automobile use and suburban expansion [D48]. The juxtaposition of Futurama and Democracity illustrates the multifaceted nature of the fair's vision, where optimism for the future coexisted with corporate agendas.

## The Garden City Movement
Another significant aspect of the fair's vision was its connection to the garden city movement, which aimed to create self-sufficient communities that combined the benefits of urban and rural living [D20]. The principles of this movement were evident in the designs presented at the fair, as they sought to address the social issues caused by industrialization and urban overcrowding [D20].

Ebenezer Howard, who proposed the garden city concept in 1898, envisioned communities that would be economically independent and promote short commutes while preserving green spaces [D20]. This vision resonated with the aspirations of fairgoers who were looking for alternatives to the crowded cities of the time. However, the garden city movement also faced criticism for its failures to distinguish between true garden cities and mundane suburban developments [D20].

## The Legacy of the Fair
The 1939 New York World's Fair left a lasting legacy on urban planning and development, influencing subsequent initiatives such as the New Towns Act in the UK and the INA-Casa plan in Italy [D20]. The revival of garden city principles after World War II demonstrated the enduring appeal of the fair's vision, as cities sought to create new communities based on Howard's ideas [D20].

Moreover, the fair's influence extended beyond the United States, with examples of garden cities emerging in various parts of the world, including Africa, Asia, and Europe [D20]. The principles of the garden city movement continue to inform contemporary urban planning, as seen in initiatives like New Urbanism, which seeks to create walkable, sustainable communities [D20].

## Comparing Then and Now
In conclusion, the 1939 New York World's Fair represented a pivotal moment in the imagination of future cities and everyday life. The visions presented at the fair were shaped by a combination of optimism for technological advancement and the interests of corporate sponsors. While Futurama and Democracity offered hopeful glimpses of the future, they also reflected the realities of a society grappling with economic challenges and the influence of corporate power.

Today, as we face new challenges such as climate change and urbanization, the lessons from the fair remain relevant. The balance between corporate interests and the public good continues to shape urban planning and development. As we envision the future, it is essential to consider whose interests are being served and how we can create sustainable, inclusive communities that reflect the aspirations of all citizens.
