# The World of Tomorrow: Visions of the Future at the 1939 New York World's Fair

## Introduction
The 1939 New York World's Fair, themed "The World of Tomorrow," was a significant cultural event that presented a vision of the future shaped by technological advancements and urban planning. This fair, particularly through its centerpiece exhibit, Futurama, sponsored by General Motors (GM), offered a glimpse into an idealized future that emphasized automation, transportation, and societal reform. However, the fair also reflected the interests of specific groups, primarily the automotive industry, while leaving out others, particularly marginalized communities. This report explores whose future was imagined at the fair, the interests it served, and the implications of these visions for contemporary society.

## Futurama: A Vision of Progress
Futurama was a large-scale model designed by Norman Bel Geddes, showcasing a proposed automated highway system that emphasized technological progress and urban planning [D19]. The exhibit aimed to inspire public enthusiasm for highway construction and urban redesign, presenting a simulated aerial journey through a model of America [D19]. This model depicted an idealized future characterized by multi-lane highways, remote-controlled vehicles, and various technologies intended for societal reform [D19]. 

The design of Futurama reflected the fair's overarching theme of "The World of Tomorrow" and was favored over other plans for its broader audience appeal, becoming a main attraction at the fair [D19]. The exhibit garnered the highest interest score from fairgoers, indicating its popularity and resonance with an audience recovering from the Great Depression [D19]. The landscape depicted in Futurama was one of prosperity and advanced urban planning, suggesting a future where technology would solve societal problems and improve quality of life [D19]. 

## The Role of General Motors
General Motors, established in 1908, was a key player in the automotive industry and the largest automaker in the United States for decades [D21]. The company's sponsorship of Futurama was not merely philanthropic; it was a strategic move to shape public perception of the automobile's role in modern life. By showcasing a future dominated by automobiles and highways, GM sought to influence urban planning and transportation policies in ways that would benefit its business interests [D21]. 

The influence of GM extended beyond the fair, as Futurama played a significant role in shaping the Federal-Aid Highway Act, which contributed to the development of the Interstate Highway System [D19]. This legislation further entrenched the automobile's central role in American life and urban development, aligning with GM's interests in promoting car culture and highway construction. 

## Exclusion and Marginalization
While Futurama and the fair presented a vision of progress and prosperity, they also reflected significant exclusions. The idealized future depicted in the exhibit primarily served the interests of the automotive industry and the middle class, often overlooking the realities faced by marginalized communities. The garden city movement, which aimed to create integrated urban environments, often resulted in exclusivity, with garden suburbs populated mainly by wealthy individuals [D20]. This exclusivity was not part of Howard's original garden city plan and was seen as a hindrance to achieving that vision [D20]. 

The emphasis on highways and suburban living in Futurama also marginalized those who could not afford automobiles or who lived in urban areas where such infrastructure was not developed. The focus on technological solutions to societal issues often ignored the systemic inequalities that existed in society, including issues of race, class, and access to resources. The fair's vision of the future was, therefore, not a universal one but rather one that reflected the interests of the powerful and affluent [D20]. 

## The Fair's Legacy and Influence
The legacy of the 1939 World's Fair and Futurama can be seen in the subsequent development of American urban landscapes. The fair's optimistic vision of the future influenced not only public sentiment but also policy decisions that shaped the built environment for decades to come. The Interstate Highway System, which emerged from the ideas presented at the fair, fundamentally altered American cities and suburbs, often at the expense of public transportation and urban neighborhoods [D19]. 

In 1964, the New York World's Fair featured a sequel to Futurama, called Futurama II, which included an immersive ride into future scenarios [D19]. This sequel attracted significant attendance, surpassing previous records set by the 1939 fair, indicating the continued fascination with the vision of a technologically advanced future [D19]. The enduring popularity of these exhibits reflects a societal desire for progress and innovation, even as the realities of urban life continued to evolve. 

## Comparison of Then and Now
When comparing the visions presented at the 1939 World's Fair with contemporary perspectives on the future, several key differences emerge. The optimism surrounding technological advancements and urban planning in the 1930s contrasts sharply with today's more nuanced understanding of the implications of such developments. While the fair celebrated the automobile and suburban living as symbols of progress, contemporary discussions often highlight the environmental and social consequences of these choices. 

Today, there is a growing awareness of the need for sustainable urban planning that prioritizes public transportation, green spaces, and equitable access to resources. The challenges of climate change, social inequality, and urban sprawl have prompted a reevaluation of the ideals presented at the fair. Modern visions of the future increasingly emphasize inclusivity, sustainability, and resilience, reflecting a broader understanding of the complexities of urban life. 

## Conclusion
The 1939 New York World's Fair and its centerpiece, Futurama, presented a vision of the future that served the interests of the automotive industry and the middle class while marginalizing other communities. The fair's optimistic portrayal of technological progress and urban planning shaped public sentiment and influenced policy decisions that continue to impact American cities today. However, the exclusionary nature of the fair's vision highlights the need for a more inclusive approach to urban development that considers the diverse needs of all communities. As we look to the future, it is essential to learn from the past and strive for a vision that prioritizes sustainability, equity, and inclusivity.
