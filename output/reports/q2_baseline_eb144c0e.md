# Whose Future Was the 'World of Tomorrow'? An Analysis of the 1939 New York World's Fair

## Introduction
The 1939 New York World's Fair, themed "The World of Tomorrow," was a significant cultural event that presented a vision of the future shaped by technological advancements and urban planning. At the heart of this fair was the Futurama exhibit, designed by Norman Bel Geddes and sponsored by General Motors (GM). This report explores the future imagined at the fair, examining whose interests were represented and whose were marginalized. By analyzing the themes of technological optimism and urban planning, we can better understand the socio-economic context of the time and the implications for various communities.

## The Vision of Futurama
Futurama was the most popular exhibit at the 1939 New York World's Fair, attracting over 30,000 visitors daily [D19]. It introduced the American public to the concept of a network of expressways, emphasizing hope for the future and aiming to solve traffic congestion [D19]. The exhibit showcased a model of a technologically advanced America, featuring over 500,000 buildings and 50,000 cars [D19]. Visitors experienced a simulated aerial journey, allowing them to view the model from an elevated position, which further enhanced the sense of optimism and progress [D19].

Bel Geddes' vision was closely tied to technological progress and aimed to reform society through urban planning [D19]. The depiction of automated highways and vast suburbs in Futurama reflected a belief in the transformative power of technology and the automobile, which was a central theme for GM, the largest automaker in the United States at the time [D21].

## Corporate Interests and Urban Planning
The sponsorship of Futurama by General Motors highlights the corporate interests that shaped the fair's vision of the future. GM, established in 1908, was known for its extensive influence in the automotive industry and had a vested interest in promoting car culture [D21]. The fair's portrayal of a future dominated by automobiles served to reinforce GM's market position and justify its business model, which included annual model changes and a pricing strategy that catered to different consumer segments [D21].

Moreover, the fair's optimistic vision aligned with GM's long-term goals, such as its announcement to end production of vehicles using internal combustion engines by 2035 [D21]. This indicates a continued corporate interest in shaping environmental regulations and public perception regarding transportation and urban development.

## Inclusion and Exclusion
While Futurama presented a hopeful vision for the future, it also reflected significant exclusions. The exhibit primarily catered to a middle-class audience, showcasing a suburban ideal that emphasized individual car ownership and a lifestyle centered around the automobile. This vision marginalized lower-income communities and those who relied on public transportation, as evidenced by GM's alleged monopolization of bus sales to dismantle streetcar systems in the U.S. between 1938 and 1950 [D21].

Additionally, the urban planning principles showcased in Futurama were influenced by the garden city movement, which aimed to create satellite communities around a central city, separated by green belts [D20]. While this concept sought to combine the benefits of urban and rural living, it also faced criticism for failing to adequately address the needs of diverse populations. Critics argued that garden cities did not distinguish between true garden cities and mundane dormitory plans, leading to economic and social challenges [D20].

The top-down approach often employed in urban planning, as exemplified by the practices of influential planners like Robert Moses, further exacerbated these exclusions. Moses' projects frequently replaced low-income housing with public housing, disproportionately impacting poor and minority communities [D43]. His focus on car dependency and large infrastructure projects drew criticism from activists who highlighted the negative consequences for marginalized populations [D43].

## The Role of Urban Planners
Urban planners play a crucial role in shaping the built environment, focusing on public welfare and efficiency [D49]. However, the planning process has often excluded residents from decision-making, leading to a lack of community representation in urban development. Sherry Arnstein's 'ladder of citizen participation' serves as a tool to assess inclusivity in urban planning, revealing the challenges of participatory approaches [D49].

The evolution of urban planning has increasingly incorporated social and environmental considerations, aiming to improve health and sustainability [D49]. Yet, the legacy of top-down planning remains, as evidenced by the ongoing housing crises in various regions, which have been exacerbated by inadequate community engagement [D49].

## Conclusion
The 1939 New York World's Fair and its Futurama exhibit presented a vision of the future that was deeply intertwined with corporate interests and technological optimism. While it showcased an idealized suburban lifestyle centered around the automobile, it also marginalized lower-income communities and those reliant on public transportation. The exclusionary practices of urban planning, exemplified by figures like Robert Moses, further highlighted the disparities in whose interests were represented in the vision of the future.

Comparing the past to the present, we see a continued struggle for inclusivity in urban planning and transportation policies. As cities grapple with issues of sustainability, social equity, and community engagement, the lessons from the 1939 fair remain relevant. The challenge lies in creating a future that serves the interests of all communities, rather than a select few, ensuring that the vision of tomorrow is inclusive and equitable.
