# The World of Tomorrow: Visions of the Future at the 1939 New York World's Fair

## Introduction
The 1939 New York World's Fair, themed "The World of Tomorrow," served as a significant cultural and technological showcase, reflecting the aspirations and anxieties of a nation recovering from the Great Depression. Among the most notable exhibits was Futurama, designed by Norman Bel Geddes and sponsored by General Motors (GM). This exhibit presented a vision of an American utopia, heavily influenced by the interests of automobile manufacturers and urban planners. This report examines whose future was imagined at the fair, the inclusivity of its vision, and the implications of its messages.

## Futurama: A Vision of Progress
Futurama introduced the concept of a network of expressways connecting the nation, showcasing a proposed solution to traffic congestion and emphasizing technological progress [D19]. The exhibit was designed to inspire public enthusiasm for street and highway planning, reflecting a vision of an American utopia regulated by cutting-edge technologies [D19]. Visitors were treated to a simulated low-flying airplane journey through a model of America, which presented a prophecy of cities and a comprehensive road system [D19]. This vision was not merely a whimsical fantasy; it was seen as a realistic glimpse of the future, appealing to an audience eager for hope and progress after the hardships of the Great Depression [D19]. 

## The Interests Behind the Vision
The vision presented in Futurama primarily reflected the interests of automobile manufacturers and urban planners [D19]. General Motors, the largest automaker in the United States, played a pivotal role in shaping this narrative. Founded in 1908 by William C. Durant, GM became a public company in 1916 and was known for its innovative marketing strategies, including annual model changes [D21]. The company's influence extended beyond mere vehicle production; it actively sought to promote a vision of America that prioritized automobile travel and the infrastructure to support it. 

Futurama was considered the 'number-one hit show' of the 1939 New York World's Fair, attracting over 30,000 visitors daily [D19]. This popularity was indicative of the broader appeal of the automobile-centric vision, which resonated with a public eager to embrace modernity and technological advancement. However, this vision was not without its critics. The focus on automobiles and highways often overlooked the needs of those who relied on public transportation or lived in urban areas that would be adversely affected by such developments. 

## Exclusion and Critique
While Futurama painted an optimistic picture of the future, it also reflected a selective vision that excluded significant portions of the population. The exhibit's emphasis on highways and suburban expansion catered primarily to middle-class Americans who could afford automobiles and suburban homes. In contrast, urban dwellers and those without access to cars were largely left out of this narrative. The focus on automobile travel and suburban living ignored the realities faced by many working-class individuals and marginalized communities, who often relied on public transportation and lived in densely populated urban areas. 

Moreover, the exhibit's portrayal of technological progress was not without its ethical implications. The automobile industry, including GM, faced criticism for its role in promoting a car-centric culture that contributed to environmental degradation and urban sprawl. Allegations surfaced that GM had monopolized bus sales to dismantle streetcar systems in the U.S. between 1938 and 1950, further marginalizing public transit users [D21]. This monopolization reflected a broader trend in which the interests of large corporations overshadowed the needs of the community, raising questions about whose future was truly being envisioned at the fair. 

## The Role of Labor and Social Movements
The context of labor movements during this period also influenced the narrative presented at the fair. The United Auto Workers (UAW) emerged as a significant labor union following the Flint sit-down strike against GM in 1936-1937, which led to the unionization of the domestic automobile industry [D21]. This struggle for workers' rights highlighted the tensions between corporate interests and the needs of laborers, who often faced precarious working conditions and economic uncertainty. The UAW's successful strike led to GM recognizing the union as the exclusive bargaining representative for its employees, marking a pivotal moment in labor history [D21]. 

However, the fair's vision of the future largely ignored these labor struggles and the voices of workers. The emphasis on technological progress and the automobile industry overshadowed the realities faced by those who built the very vehicles that were celebrated at the fair. This exclusion raises critical questions about the inclusivity of the vision presented at the fair and whose interests were prioritized in the narrative of progress. 

## Urban Planning and the Future
The fair's vision of the future was also influenced by contemporary urban planning movements. The garden city movement, which promoted satellite communities around a central city, aimed to combine the benefits of countryside and city living while avoiding their disadvantages [D20]. This concept, proposed by Ebenezer Howard in 1898, sought to create a harmonious balance between urban and rural living. However, the implementation of such ideas often favored suburban expansion at the expense of urban centers, further marginalizing those who lived in cities. 

Urban planning has historically been a top-down approach, with decisions made by planners and policymakers often without adequate community involvement [D49]. While the fair showcased innovative ideas for urban development, it also reflected a tendency to prioritize the interests of corporations and affluent communities over those of marginalized populations. The vision of the future presented at the fair was thus a reflection of the prevailing power dynamics in society, where the voices of the powerful often drowned out those of the less privileged. 

## Conclusion: Comparing Then and Now
The 1939 New York World's Fair and its vision of the future serve as a lens through which to examine the complexities of progress and inclusion. While the fair celebrated technological advancements and the promise of a better tomorrow, it also revealed significant exclusions and power imbalances. The vision of the future was largely shaped by the interests of automobile manufacturers and urban planners, often at the expense of marginalized communities and laborers.

In contemporary society, similar themes persist. The ongoing debates about urban planning, transportation infrastructure, and environmental sustainability echo the tensions present at the fair. As cities grapple with the challenges of climate change and social inequality, the need for inclusive and equitable visions of the future remains paramount. The lessons from the 1939 World's Fair remind us that progress should not come at the cost of exclusion, and that a truly inclusive vision of the future must consider the needs and voices of all members of society.
