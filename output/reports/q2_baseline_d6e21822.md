# Whose Future Was the 'World of Tomorrow'? An Analysis of the 1939 New York World's Fair

## Introduction
The 1939 New York World's Fair, themed "The World of Tomorrow," was a monumental event that showcased a vision of the future steeped in optimism and technological advancement. This fair was not just a display of innovation; it was a reflection of the social, economic, and political climate of the time, particularly in the context of the Great Depression. The fair's most notable exhibit, Futurama, designed by Norman Bel Geddes and sponsored by General Motors, presented a future dominated by automobiles and suburban living. However, this vision was not inclusive; it primarily represented the interests of the middle class and affluent Americans while marginalizing lower-income populations and diverse community voices. This report will explore the future imagined at the fair, the interests it served, and the implications of its exclusionary practices.

## Futurama: A Vision of Progress
Futurama was the centerpiece of the 1939 World's Fair, showcasing a model of America 20 years into the future, specifically around 1959-1960. The exhibit featured advanced technology, including an automated highway system, which emphasized hope for the future and aimed to inspire public support for urban planning initiatives [D19]. It depicted an idealized built environment with 500,000 buildings, 1 million trees, and 50,000 cars, all designed around principles of safety, comfort, speed, and economy in highway design [D19]. The aerial journey through Futurama allowed spectators to experience a simulated future landscape, highlighting the technological progress that was envisioned to improve daily life [D19]. 

The design of Futurama resonated with an audience recovering from the Great Depression, reflecting a collective longing for prosperity and stability [D19]. The exhibit was a hit, ranked as the most interesting by a neutral survey, indicating its broad appeal and the public's desire for a brighter future [D19]. However, while Futurama painted a picture of progress and innovation, it was also a product of corporate sponsorship, specifically from General Motors, which had vested interests in promoting automobile-centric urban planning [D19]. 

## The Garden City Movement: An Alternative Vision
In contrast to the automobile-dominated vision of Futurama, the garden city movement, initiated by Ebenezer Howard in 1898, sought to provide an alternative to overcrowded urban environments. Howard's concept aimed to create self-sufficient communities that combined the benefits of both urban and rural living [D20]. The garden city model was designed to attract residents from crowded cities and isolated countrysides, promoting better living conditions for the working class [D20]. However, the funding for these projects primarily came from wealthy investors, which limited the involvement of working-class cooperatives and ultimately skewed the benefits toward the middle class [D20]. 

The first garden city, Letchworth, was intended to include affordable housing but ended up attracting mostly skilled middle-class residents, highlighting the limitations of the model [D20]. Critics of the garden city movement argue that it can lead to ghetto communities and increased crime, as well as environmental degradation due to its failure to promote urban density [D20]. Despite its intentions, the garden city movement is often viewed as an urban design experiment that has faced significant failures [D20]. 

## Exclusionary Practices in Urban Planning
The 1939 World's Fair and its exhibits, including Futurama, exemplified the top-down approach that has historically characterized urban planning. This approach often excludes local residents from decision-making processes, leading to a lack of representation for marginalized communities [D49]. Sherry Arnstein's 'ladder of citizen participation' serves as a framework to assess inclusivity in urban planning, revealing that most processes tend to prioritize the interests of the affluent while sidelining those of lower-income populations [D49]. 

City council meetings, which are a primary source of engagement between city officials and residents, often fail to capture the diverse voices of the community [D49]. This exclusion is particularly evident in the planning of the 1939 World's Fair, where the vision presented was largely shaped by corporate sponsors like General Motors, reflecting their interests in promoting automobile dependency and suburbanization [D19]. As a result, the fair's vision of the future primarily served the middle class and affluent Americans, leaving marginalized communities and lower-income populations largely unrepresented [D49]. 

## The Role of Corporations in Shaping Urban Futures
The influence of corporations, particularly automobile manufacturers like General Motors and Chrysler, on urban planning cannot be overstated. General Motors' sponsorship of Futurama was a strategic move to promote its vision of a future dominated by cars and highways, which aligned with its business interests [D19]. The company's alleged monopolization of bus sales to dismantle streetcar systems in the U.S. further illustrates how corporate interests shaped public transportation and urban landscapes [D21]. 

Chrysler, founded by Walter Chrysler in 1925, aimed to provide advanced, well-engineered cars at affordable prices, introducing innovations that would appeal to the growing middle class [D07]. The company's rise to become the second-largest U.S. automobile manufacturer by 1936 was indicative of the broader trend of automobile dependency that was being promoted at the fair [D07]. The Flint sit-down strike of 1936-1937, which transformed the United Auto Workers (UAW) into a major labor union, highlighted the tensions between labor rights and corporate interests in the automobile industry [D21]. 

## The Legacy of the 1939 World's Fair
The legacy of the 1939 World's Fair and its vision of the future continues to influence contemporary urban planning. The fair's emphasis on automobile-centric development laid the groundwork for subsequent Federal-Aid Highway Act bills, contributing to the establishment of the Interstate Highway System [D19]. The ideals presented at the fair, particularly those embodied in Futurama, have been echoed in later urban planning initiatives, including the General Motors pavilion at the 1964 New York World's Fair, which featured a sequel to Futurama [D19]. 

However, the exclusionary practices that characterized the planning of the 1939 fair have persisted in modern urban planning. Recent debates emphasize the need for more inclusive approaches that involve diverse community voices in decision-making processes [D49]. While urban planning aims to improve health and well-being, it often fails to adequately represent the interests of marginalized populations [D49]. 

## Conclusion: Comparing Then and Now
The vision of the future presented at the 1939 New York World's Fair, particularly through the Futurama exhibit, was a reflection of the interests of the middle class and affluent Americans. It showcased a technologically advanced world dominated by automobiles and suburban living, while largely excluding lower-income populations and marginalized communities. The garden city movement offered an alternative vision, but its implementation often favored the middle class and failed to address the needs of the working poor. 

Today, urban planning continues to grapple with issues of inclusivity and representation. While there is a growing recognition of the importance of involving diverse community voices in decision-making processes, the legacy of exclusion from the 1939 World's Fair serves as a reminder of the challenges that persist in creating equitable urban environments. As we envision the future, it is crucial to learn from the past and strive for a more inclusive approach that serves the interests of all community members, not just the affluent few.
