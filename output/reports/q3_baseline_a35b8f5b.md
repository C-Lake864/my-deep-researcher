# The Vision of Futurama and Its Impact on Post-War America

## Introduction
The post-World War II era in America was marked by significant changes in urban planning and transportation. One of the most influential visions of this time was presented by General Motors in their exhibit, Futurama, which showcased an idealized future of highways and automobiles. This report explores how Futurama's vision connected to the actual highways and suburbs that were built in America after the war, examining the interests it served and the implications for society.

## Futurama: A Vision of Highways and Cars
Futurama introduced the concept of a network of expressways that would connect the nation, aiming to inspire public enthusiasm for highway planning and construction [D19]. The exhibit featured a model of a motorway system designed to address traffic congestion and accommodate the anticipated growth in automotive use [D19]. Designed by Norman Bel Geddes, the model emphasized safety, comfort, speed, and economy in highway construction, reflecting the desires of a society eager for progress [D19]. 

The Futurama model included a 14-lane multi-speed interstate highway, predicting an American utopia supported by advanced technologies such as remote-controlled vehicles [D19]. This vision not only captured the imagination of the public but also aligned with the federal initiatives that were emerging to improve the nation’s infrastructure. The Federal Aid Road Act of 1916, which initiated federal funding for highway construction, laid the groundwork for future highway systems [D28]. E.J. Mehren's proposal for a 50,000-mile national highway system in 1918 and the subsequent Federal Aid Highway Act of 1921 aimed to create a national road grid, setting the stage for the Interstate Highway System [D28]. 

## The Development of the Interstate Highway System
By the late 1930s, planning for superhighways was underway, aligning with Futurama's vision of advanced highway systems [D28]. President Roosevelt's 1938 marking of eight superhighway corridors for study was a pivotal moment that eventually led to the establishment of the Interstate Highway System in 1956 [D28]. This system, characterized by controlled-access highways, was designed to have unified standards for construction and signage, a significant improvement over earlier highway systems [D28]. 

The construction of the Interstate Highway System had profound implications, including the displacement of many neighborhoods as part of urban renewal programs [D28]. With a total length of 48,890 miles, the system accounts for about a quarter of all vehicle miles driven in the U.S., illustrating its central role in American transportation [D28]. The construction cost was approximately $114 billion, equivalent to $634 billion in 2024, indicating the scale of investment in this infrastructure [D28]. 

## The Rise of Suburbia: Levittown as a Case Study
Parallel to the development of highways was the rise of suburban communities, epitomized by Levittown, the first planned community built by Levitt & Sons in 1947 [D29]. Levittown's design aimed at affordability for returning GIs, facilitated by federal housing subsidies, and utilized mass production techniques inspired by military housing methods [D29]. The community expanded rapidly, reflecting the post-war housing boom and suburbanization trend in America [D29]. 

Levittown was characterized by its reliance on road transportation, lacking passenger rail service and instead being served by bus systems and nearby airports [D29]. Major roads, such as NY 24, also known as Hempstead Turnpike, became the busiest routes in Levittown, featuring local businesses and congestion that mirrored suburban traffic patterns [D29]. This reliance on highways and cars was a direct manifestation of the vision presented in Futurama, where the automobile was central to daily life and community structure. 

## The Influence of Urban Planning Concepts
The development of Levittown and similar suburbs can also be linked to urban planning concepts such as Howard's garden city model, which aimed to relieve urban congestion and provide an alternative to crowded cities [D20]. The garden city model proposed self-sufficient communities linked by roads, a principle that resonates with the design of modern suburbs [D20]. Letchworth, the first garden city, attracted residents through low taxes and more space, reflecting the appeal of suburban living post-war [D20]. 

The organic design of Letchworth, which deviated from Howard's original plan, indicates a flexibility in urban planning that can be seen in suburban developments like Levittown [D20]. The principles of the garden city influenced urban design in the early 20th century, including post-war suburban developments, showcasing a blend of ideals that shaped the American landscape. 

## Conclusion
In conclusion, GM's Futurama vision of highways and cars significantly influenced the actual highways and suburbs built in America after World War II. The exhibit not only captured the aspirations of a nation eager for progress but also served the interests of automobile manufacturers and suburban developers. Today, while the vision of interconnected highways remains relevant, the consequences of such developments, including urban sprawl and environmental concerns, prompt a reevaluation of the ideals that once drove American infrastructure. The contrast between the optimistic visions of the past and the challenges of the present highlights the need for a more sustainable and inclusive approach to urban planning and transportation.
