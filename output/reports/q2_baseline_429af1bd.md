# The World of Tomorrow: Visions of the Future at the 1939 New York World's Fair

## Introduction
The 1939 New York World's Fair, themed 'The World of Tomorrow,' presented a vision of the future that was both aspirational and reflective of the socio-economic context of its time. This fair was a significant cultural event that showcased technological advancements and urban planning concepts, particularly through the Futurama exhibit designed by Norman Bel Geddes. However, the fair also served specific interests, primarily those of corporate entities like General Motors, and excluded various demographic groups from its utopian visions. This report explores whose future was imagined at the fair, the inclusivity of its visions, and the implications for urban planning and societal development.

## Futurama: A Vision of Technological Progress
Futurama was the centerpiece of the 1939 New York World's Fair, showcasing a model of America 20 years into the future, specifically in 1959–1960 [D19]. The exhibit featured an automated highway system, emphasizing technological progress and urban planning as key components of a prosperous future [D19]. The large-scale model included advanced transportation concepts, such as multi-lane highways and remote-controlled vehicles, suggesting a utopian future regulated by technology [D19]. This vision was particularly appealing to an audience recovering from the Great Depression, as it promised efficiency, safety, and a better quality of life through technological advancements [D19].

Bel Geddes' design was not merely an artistic endeavor; it aimed to inspire public support for highway construction and urban planning, reflecting a broader agenda of promoting automobile culture [D19]. The popularity of Futurama, attracting over 30,000 visitors daily, indicated a strong public interest in the future it presented [D19]. However, this vision was closely tied to the interests of General Motors, which sponsored the exhibit and sought to influence public policy regarding transportation and urban development [D19].

## The Interests Served by the Fair
The 1939 World's Fair was heavily influenced by corporate interests, particularly those of General Motors, which was the largest global automaker during this period [D21]. The fair served as a platform for GM to showcase its vision of the future, which was intrinsically linked to the promotion of automobile dependency and highway construction [D19]. This alignment of corporate interests with public policy had significant implications for urban planning and transportation in the United States, as evidenced by the influence of Futurama on multiple Federal-Aid Highway Act bills, contributing to the development of the Interstate Highway System [D19].

General Motors' dominance in the automotive industry was further solidified by its strategies, such as annual model changes and a pricing structure that positioned its brands across different market segments [D21]. This approach not only catered to a diverse consumer base but also ensured that the company remained at the forefront of technological innovation in the automotive sector [D21]. However, the focus on automobiles and highways often came at the expense of alternative forms of transportation and urban designs that prioritized public welfare and environmental sustainability.

## Inclusion and Exclusion in the Vision of Tomorrow
While the fair presented an optimistic vision of the future, it also reflected significant exclusions. The utopian model showcased in Futurama primarily served the interests of the middle and upper classes, who could afford the lifestyle associated with automobile ownership and suburban living [D19]. The design of the model cities prioritized safety and efficiency, but it did not adequately address the needs of lower-income populations or marginalized communities [D19].

The garden city movement, which influenced urban planning during this period, aimed to create self-sufficient communities that combined the benefits of urban and rural living [D20]. However, the implementation of these concepts often led to unintended consequences, such as the development of ghetto communities and increased crime due to poor design and lack of resources [D20]. Additionally, the reliance on wealthy investors for funding garden cities compromised the original vision of inclusivity, making these developments unaffordable for blue-collar workers [D20].

Urban planning during this era followed a predominantly top-down approach, which often excluded residents from decision-making processes [D49]. Sherry Arnstein's 'ladder of citizen participation' illustrates the limited opportunities for community involvement in urban planning, highlighting the disconnect between planners and the communities they served [D49]. This lack of inclusivity was evident in the critiques of powerful urban planners like Robert Moses, whose projects often displaced low-income housing and negatively impacted poor and minority communities [D43].

## The Legacy of the Fair and Its Vision
The legacy of the 1939 World's Fair and its vision of the future can be seen in the ongoing development of urban landscapes and transportation systems in the United States. The emphasis on automobile dependency and suburban expansion has shaped the way cities are designed and how communities interact with their environments. However, the consequences of these developments have also led to significant challenges, including urban sprawl, environmental degradation, and social inequities.

The environmental movement has criticized the garden city model and the focus on automobile-centric planning for failing to promote urban density and sustainability [D20]. Critics argue that the exclusionary practices of urban planning have contributed to housing crises in various regions, as participatory planning efforts have often been inadequate in addressing the needs of marginalized populations [D49].

In contrast to the top-down approaches of the past, recent trends in urban planning emphasize community involvement and interdisciplinary collaboration [D49]. The shift towards participatory planning reflects a growing recognition of the importance of inclusivity and social equity in shaping urban environments. Jane Jacobs' work in the 1960s, which advocated for livable streets and community perspectives, has influenced contemporary urban planning practices [D49].

## Conclusion
The 1939 New York World's Fair, with its theme of 'The World of Tomorrow,' presented a vision of the future that was both aspirational and reflective of the socio-economic realities of its time. While the fair showcased technological advancements and urban planning concepts that promised a better quality of life, it also served the interests of corporate entities like General Motors and excluded marginalized communities from its utopian visions. The legacy of the fair continues to influence urban planning and transportation systems today, highlighting the need for inclusivity and sustainability in shaping the future. As society grapples with the challenges of urbanization and environmental degradation, the lessons learned from the past can inform more equitable and participatory approaches to urban planning.
