# Imagining the Future: The 1939 New York World's Fair and Its Visions of Urban Life

## Introduction
The 1939 New York World's Fair was a monumental event that showcased the aspirations and anxieties of a nation emerging from the Great Depression. With the theme "The World of Tomorrow," the fair served as a platform for various visions of the future, particularly in urban planning and everyday life. This report will explore how the fair imagined future cities and lifestyles, focusing on the contrasting visions of its organizers and the corporations involved, particularly General Motors. By examining the exhibits, especially the Futurama and the garden city concepts, we can understand the interests they served and the implications for contemporary urban planning.

## Futurama: A Vision of Automated Highways
One of the most significant attractions of the 1939 World's Fair was the Futurama exhibit, designed by Norman Bel Geddes and sponsored by General Motors. Futurama presented a vision of an automated highway system that was not only achievable but also influential in shaping future urban planning [D19]. The exhibit introduced the American public to the concept of a network of expressways, emphasizing technological progress as a solution to traffic congestion [D19].

Bel Geddes' model illustrated a comprehensive motorway system designed for safety, comfort, speed, and economy, featuring over 500,000 buildings and 50,000 cars, predicting an American utopia powered by advanced technologies [D19]. The exhibit included an 18-minute simulated aerial journey that allowed visitors to view the future city from a unique perspective, further immersing them in this imagined landscape of 1960 [D19].

Futurama was not only the main attraction of the fair but also considered the "number-one hit show," attracting over 30,000 visitors daily and receiving a high interest rating compared to other exhibits [D19]. The success of Futurama can be attributed to its realistic and immediate ideas, resonating with an audience eager for hope and progress after the hardships of the Great Depression [D19].

## The Garden City Movement: A Different Vision
While Futurama presented a vision centered around technological advancement and urban sprawl, another significant concept showcased at the fair was the garden city movement. Proposed by Ebenezer Howard in 1898, the garden city aimed to combine the benefits of countryside and city life while avoiding their disadvantages [D20]. This model promoted satellite communities around a central city, separated by green belts, and was intended to be self-sufficient, linked to the central city by road and rail [D20].

The garden city concept was exemplified in the fair's Perisphere, which housed a diorama called Democracity. This diorama depicted a utopian city-of-the-future, aligning with the fair's overarching theme [D48]. Visitors viewed Democracity from above on a moving sidewalk, which provided a dynamic perspective of the envisioned urban life [D48].

Howard's diagrams presented a concentric city pattern with open spaces and radial boulevards, emphasizing a harmonious blend of nature and urban living [D20]. However, the implementation of garden cities faced challenges, as seen in the First Garden City, Letchworth, which struggled to remain affordable for blue-collar workers [D20]. Critics of garden cities have pointed out that they can lead to social issues, such as crime and ghetto communities, and have been critiqued for not supporting urban density [D20].

## Contrasting Interests: Corporations vs. Urban Planners
The visions presented at the 1939 World's Fair reflected differing interests between corporate sponsors and urban planners. General Motors, through Futurama, promoted a future reliant on automobiles and highways, which aligned with their business interests in the automotive industry [D19]. The company's decision to scrap original plans for their exhibit in favor of Bel Geddes' design demonstrates a strategic move to appeal to the public's desire for progress and innovation [D19].

In contrast, the garden city movement, while also forward-thinking, emphasized sustainability and community over the individualistic nature of car-centric urban planning. This vision sought to create integrated communities that fostered social interaction and economic independence, diverging from the suburban model promoted by corporations like General Motors [D20].

The fair's exhibits, therefore, served as a battleground for competing visions of the future. While Futurama illustrated a world dominated by automobiles and sprawling suburbs, the garden city concept offered an alternative that prioritized green spaces and community living. The success of Futurama in attracting visitors highlighted the public's inclination towards technological optimism, even as it raised questions about the long-term implications of such a vision [D19][D20].

## Lasting Impact and Legacy
The influence of the 1939 World's Fair extended beyond its immediate impact, shaping urban planning and development in the decades that followed. Futurama's vision of an automated highway system influenced the Federal-Aid Highway Act bills, ultimately leading to the establishment of the Interstate Highway System [D19]. This monumental infrastructure project transformed American cities and suburbs, reinforcing the automobile-centric model of urban life.

Conversely, the garden city principles continued to inspire urban planning efforts worldwide. The movement's influence can be seen in the design of colonial and post-colonial capitals, such as New Delhi and Canberra, as well as in contemporary urban planning initiatives that seek to integrate green spaces into urban environments [D20]. For instance, Singapore has successfully incorporated garden city principles into its urban planning, enhancing greenery and nature in community development [D20].

Despite the initial optimism surrounding both visions, the realities of urban development have often deviated from their ideals. Many modern garden cities have devolved into dormitory suburbs, lacking the integrated community life that Howard envisioned [D20]. Similarly, the rise of urban sprawl has led to increased traffic congestion and environmental concerns, challenging the sustainability of the automobile-dependent model promoted by Futurama.

## Conclusion: Comparing Then and Now
The 1939 New York World's Fair presented two contrasting visions of the future: one that embraced technological progress and the automobile as the centerpiece of urban life, and another that sought to harmonize urban living with nature through the garden city concept. While Futurama captured the imagination of a nation eager for progress, it also laid the groundwork for an urban landscape characterized by sprawl and dependence on automobiles. In contrast, the garden city movement offered a more sustainable and community-oriented approach, though it faced its own challenges in implementation.

Today, as cities grapple with the consequences of past planning decisions, there is a renewed interest in the principles of the garden city movement and sustainable urban development. Contemporary movements like New Urbanism and Principles of Intelligent Urbanism draw inspiration from the garden city concept, emphasizing walkability, mixed-use development, and the integration of green spaces into urban environments [D20]. As we reflect on the visions presented at the 1939 World's Fair, it becomes clear that the future of urban life remains a complex interplay of technological innovation, social needs, and environmental considerations.
