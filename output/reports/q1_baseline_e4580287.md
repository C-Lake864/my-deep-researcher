# Imagining the Future: The 1939 New York World's Fair and Its Visions of Urban Life

## Introduction
The 1939 New York World's Fair was a monumental event that showcased the aspirations and anxieties of a nation emerging from the Great Depression. It was a platform for various visions of the future, particularly through the lens of urban planning and daily life. Central to this exposition was the Futurama exhibit, designed by Norman Bel Geddes and sponsored by General Motors, which presented a utopian vision of automated highways and sprawling suburbs. This report explores how the fair imagined the future city and everyday life, examining the differing interests of the organizers and corporate sponsors.

## Futurama: A Vision of the Future
Futurama was the centerpiece of the 1939 World's Fair, attracting over 30,000 visitors daily and receiving high praise from both the public and critics [D19]. The exhibit presented a model of America in the year 1960, featuring over 500,000 buildings and 50,000 cars, predicting a future dominated by increased vehicle use [D19]. The vision emphasized safety, comfort, speed, and economy, proposing solutions to traffic congestion through an extensive network of automated highways [D19]. This prototype of an automated highway system was not merely a fantasy; it influenced subsequent Federal-Aid Highway Act bills that ultimately led to the creation of the Interstate Highway System [D19].

Spectators experienced an 18-minute simulated aerial journey through this model city, which highlighted technological progress and the promise of a prosperous future [D19]. The exhibit resonated with an audience recovering from economic hardship, reflecting their aspirations for a better life. Bel Geddes' ideas were seen as realistic and immediate, capturing the imagination of a society eager for innovation and improvement [D19].

## The Role of Corporations
The involvement of corporations, particularly General Motors, shaped the narrative of the fair. General Motors favored Bel Geddes' design for Futurama over other proposals due to its broader appeal [D19]. The company's sponsorship was not merely philanthropic; it was a strategic move to promote the automobile as a symbol of progress and modernity. Ford Motor Company, another key player in the automotive industry, also showcased its innovations but did not achieve the same level of acclaim as Futurama [D19].

Ford's introduction of large-scale manufacturing methods, known as Fordism, revolutionized car production and made automobiles more accessible to the average American [D18]. However, the company's exhibit did not capture the public's imagination in the same way as Futurama, which was seen as the 'number-one hit show' of the fair [D19]. This disparity highlights how corporate interests influenced the portrayal of future urban life, with General Motors effectively using the fair to promote its vision of a car-centric society.

## Architectural Innovations: The Trylon and Perisphere
The architectural highlights of the fair, the Trylon and Perisphere, further exemplified the optimistic vision of the future. Designed by Wallace Harrison and J. Andre Fouilhoux, these monumental structures symbolized progress and innovation [D48]. The Perisphere housed a diorama called Democracity, depicting a utopian city of the future, which was viewed from above on a moving sidewalk [D48]. This immersive experience reinforced the fair's narrative of a technologically advanced and harmonious urban environment.

The Trylon, a spire-shaped structure, was connected to the Perisphere by the world's longest escalator at the time, emphasizing the fair's commitment to showcasing modern engineering [D48]. The names 'Trylon' and 'Perisphere' reflect their architectural significance and the fair's ambition to present a new vision for urban life. These structures served as physical manifestations of the ideals promoted by the fair, encapsulating the hopes of a society looking towards a brighter future.

## The Garden City Movement
While Futurama and the fair's architectural innovations presented a vision of sprawling suburbs and automated highways, the garden city movement offered an alternative perspective on urban planning. Founded by Ebenezer Howard in 1898, the garden city concept aimed to combine the benefits of urban and rural living, promoting economically independent cities with short commutes and countryside preservation [D20].

The principles of the garden city movement influenced various urban designs globally, including colonial and post-colonial capitals. However, critics argue that the garden city model can lead to ghettoization and crime due to its design, which often separates communities from urban centers [D20]. The environmental movement has also critiqued the garden city model for promoting suburban sprawl instead of urban density [D20].

Despite these criticisms, the garden city concept remains relevant in contemporary urban planning discussions, as seen in movements like New Urbanism, which originated from garden city principles [D20]. This juxtaposition of visions—Futurama's automated highways versus the garden city’s integrated urban life—highlights the differing interests at play during the fair.

## Whose Interests Were Served?
The 1939 New York World's Fair served multiple interests, primarily those of corporate sponsors like General Motors and Ford, who sought to promote their products and visions of the future. The fair's focus on technological progress and consumerism aligned with the interests of these corporations, which aimed to position the automobile as an essential part of modern life. The portrayal of a car-centric future was not just a reflection of societal aspirations but also a marketing strategy to boost automobile sales and influence urban planning policies.

In contrast, the garden city movement represented a more holistic approach to urban living, emphasizing sustainability and community. However, this vision was often overshadowed by the dominant narrative of technological progress and consumerism presented at the fair. The interests of the corporate sponsors and the ideals of the garden city movement were at odds, reflecting broader societal tensions between economic growth and sustainable living.

## Conclusion: Comparing Then and Now
The visions of the future presented at the 1939 New York World's Fair continue to resonate today, albeit in different forms. The fair's emphasis on technological progress and the automobile as a symbol of modernity has shaped urban planning and development throughout the 20th century. However, contemporary discussions around urban living increasingly prioritize sustainability, community, and the integration of green spaces, reflecting a shift in societal values.

While the fair imagined a future dominated by cars and sprawling suburbs, today's urban planners are more inclined to consider the principles of the garden city movement, advocating for walkable communities and public transportation. This evolution in thinking highlights the ongoing struggle to balance economic interests with the need for sustainable and inclusive urban environments. As we look to the future, the lessons from the 1939 World's Fair remind us of the importance of considering whose interests are served in our visions for urban life.
