# Whose Future Was the 'World of Tomorrow'? An Analysis of the 1939 New York World's Fair

## Introduction
The 1939 New York World's Fair, themed "The World of Tomorrow," presented a vision of the future that was both optimistic and technologically advanced. This exposition, particularly through its Futurama exhibit, showcased a future dominated by automobiles, automated highways, and urban planning that promised comfort and efficiency. However, this vision was not universally inclusive; it reflected the interests of specific corporate entities, particularly General Motors (GM), and left out significant segments of the population. This report will explore whose future was imagined at the fair, the stakeholders involved, and the implications of the presented visions.

## The Vision of Futurama
Futurama, designed by Norman Bel Geddes and sponsored by General Motors, was the centerpiece of the 1939 fair, attracting over 30,000 visitors daily [D19]. The exhibit showcased a large-scale model of America, complete with advanced transportation concepts such as multi-lane highways and remote-controlled vehicles [D19]. This vision emphasized technological progress and urban planning, aiming to inspire public support for highway construction [D19]. The simulated aerial journey allowed spectators to view the model from an elevated position, highlighting the interconnectedness of urban and transportation planning [D19]. 

Bel Geddes' vision was not just about transportation; it encompassed a broader societal reform through technology, reflecting an optimism for the future [D19]. The exhibit presented a comprehensive road system serving cities, towns, and countrysides, which resonated with an audience recovering from the Great Depression [D19]. However, this vision primarily served the interests of the automobile industry and its stakeholders, particularly GM, which was the largest global automaker at the time [D21].

## Corporate Interests and Influence
General Motors, founded in 1908, was a significant player in shaping the automotive landscape of the United States [D21]. Under the leadership of figures like William C. Durant and Alfred P. Sloan Jr., GM introduced innovations such as the first electric starter and annual model changes that positioned its brands competitively [D21]. The company's influence extended beyond manufacturing; it actively sought to shape government regulations on environmental standards and monopolized bus sales to dismantle streetcar systems, prioritizing profit over public transport accessibility [D21]. 

The Futurama exhibit was a reflection of GM's corporate interests, designed to promote a future where automobiles were central to American life. This focus on automotive progress and infrastructure planning ultimately influenced legislation leading to the Interstate Highway System, showcasing the fair's impact on future infrastructure [D19]. The popularity of Futurama set attendance records, indicating its significant cultural impact and the extent to which corporate interests could shape public perception of the future [D19].

## Exclusion and Marginalization
While the fair presented a forward-looking vision, it also excluded significant segments of the population. The focus on automobiles and highways inherently marginalized those who could not afford cars or who lived in areas not served by the envisioned infrastructure. The emphasis on technological progress and urban planning reflected a middle-class perspective, overlooking the realities faced by lower-income communities and individuals without access to automobiles. 

Moreover, the labor movements of the time, particularly the rise of the United Auto Workers (UAW), were largely absent from the fair's narrative. The Flint sit-down strike (1936-1937) had transformed the UAW into a major labor union, yet the struggles of workers for recognition and better conditions were overshadowed by the fair's celebration of corporate achievements [D21]. The UAW's efforts to gain bargaining power and recognition were significant in the context of the automotive industry, yet they were not represented in the optimistic visions presented at the fair [D21].

## The Legacy of Futurama
The legacy of Futurama extended beyond the fair itself. The 1964 New York World's Fair featured a sequel to Futurama, indicating the lasting interest in the original exhibit and its vision of the future [D19]. However, the implications of the fair's vision have been mixed. While it inspired infrastructure development and reflected a period of optimism, it also contributed to urban sprawl and environmental challenges that would become apparent in the decades following the fair.

The duality of technological progress is evident in GM's innovations, such as the development of leaded gasoline, which had harmful health implications [D21]. This reflects a broader theme of the fair: while the future was imagined as bright and progressive, it often came at a cost to public health and safety, a concern that would later be highlighted by consumer advocates like Ralph Nader [D21].

## Conclusion: Then and Now
The 1939 New York World's Fair presented a vision of the future that was both aspirational and exclusionary. It served the interests of corporate entities like General Motors, promoting a future dominated by automobiles and technological progress while marginalizing those who did not fit into this narrative. The fair's legacy is a reminder of the complexities of progress; while it inspired significant infrastructure developments, it also contributed to social and environmental challenges that continue to resonate today.

In comparing the visions of then and now, it is evident that while technology continues to advance, the inclusivity of these visions remains a critical concern. Today's discussions around urban planning, transportation, and sustainability must consider the voices and needs of all communities, ensuring that the future is not just a reflection of corporate interests but a shared vision that benefits society as a whole.
